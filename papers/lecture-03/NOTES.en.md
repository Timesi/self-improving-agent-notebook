## Lecture theme

The core problem this lecture solves: **generation is easy, verification is hard**. When an LLM reasons over many steps, autoregressive generation has no mechanism to look back and correct; one intermediate error is enough to ruin the whole solution. Even when the model can sample a correct solution (Pass@K is high), picking that solution out remains the bottleneck — the generation–verification gap. This lecture teaches the agent to let "a model check a model": train an independent verifier (reward model / verifier) to judge candidates, then do best-of-N search at test time.

Why it sits after L2 and before L4: the previous lecture (test-time compute) built the intuition of "sample more + vote", but self-consistency majority vote plateaus or even degrades, so we need a stronger "selector"; this lecture builds that selector. A verifier (reward model) is also the core source of the reward signal in L6 reinforcement learning (GRPO/PPO), and the prototype of "critic / self-judgment" in later agent loops. L4's "code-execution feedback" is another kind of verification signal (execution results rather than language judgments) and complements this lecture.

Pedagogically the lecture answers four nested questions: what a verifier is and how to train it (Cobbe) → whether judging only the outcome is enough, or we should judge the process (Lightman's ORM vs PRM) → process labels are expensive, can they be automatic (Math-Shepherd) → a single verifier is still weak, how to combine many weak verifiers into a strong one (Weaver).

## Close reading

### Paper 1: Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems (arXiv:2110.14168, cobbe-verifiers.pdf)
- **Core idea**: OpenAI walks on two legs — release the GSM8K dataset, and train a "verifier" to judge model-generated candidate solutions; at test time sample many solutions and pick the one the verifier scores highest. Motivation: multi-step math reasoning is extremely sensitive to single-point errors, and an autoregressive model cannot self-correct while generating; scaling by finetuning a larger model is a poor curve (extrapolation would need 10^16 parameters or two more orders of magnitude of data to reach 80%). Verification is a more scaling-friendly path. Verification is simpler than generation (discrimination < generation), and the verifier is independent of the generator, so it naturally supports the "sample many" option.
- **Key formulas / algorithms**:
  - GSM8K: 8.5K problems (7.5K train / 1K test), grade-school level, 2–8 steps, natural-language solutions, deliberately diverse language (estimated fraction of destructive errors <2%).
  - Verifier training pipeline (three stages): ① finetune the generator on the train set for 2 epochs (more than that collapses diversity and overconfidence); ② sample 100 candidate solutions per training problem from the generator, auto-label positive/negative by "final answer == correct answer"; ③ train the verifier for 1 epoch. Generator and verifier are **two independent networks**, so the generator does not overfit the answers.
  - Verifier objective: joint loss = language-modeling loss + verification loss (MSE). The verification head is implemented as bias+gain on a special-token logit from the LM's last-layer unembed (does not interfere with ordinary-token LM).
  - token-level vs solution-level: solution-level predicts correctness once at the end of the whole solution; token-level predicts after **every token** (viewable as a token-level value function); at test time the last token's score is the solution score.
  - Calculator annotations: training corpus injects arithmetic as `20 + 10 = <<20+10=30>> 30`; at test time, on `<<...>>`, `eval` overwrites model sampling to reduce arithmetic errors.
  - Test: sample 100 solutions per problem (T=0.7) → verifier scores → take the highest; can also majority-vote the final answers of the top-k highest-scoring solutions.
- **Key experimental results**:
  - 6B verification ≈ finetuned 175B, equivalent to about a **30× model-size lift**; the verification curve vs training data is also clearly steeper (largest advantage at 8K data scale; verification is not worth it at small data).
  - token-level eventually beats solution-level and overfits less; the joint LM objective is a strict improvement.
  - large generator + small verifier ≫ small generator + large verifier — the verifier uses relatively coarse heuristics to separate solutions.
  - test@100 falls sharply with epoch (overconfidence → poor sample coverage), so sampling uses the 2-epoch model.
  - Performance peaks at 400 candidates per problem, then falls (adversarial solutions that fool the verifier appear); at 100 candidates, voting the top 3–5 is best.
  - dropout 0.2 is significant regularization for both finetuning and verification. Dropping intermediate steps and emitting the answer directly falls from 20.6% to 5.2% (intermediate reasoning steps are necessary).
- **Relation to the lecture theme**: the foundation of this lecture. Establishes the "verification + best-of-N" frame, automatic outcome labels, and a token-level value function. This paper's solution-level verifier ≈ later ORM; the token-level verifier already brings in "scores distributed by position", a transition toward process supervision (each token's target is still whole-solution correctness; note the difference from PRM).
- **Demo-ready code points**: on a mini arithmetic dataset, train an outcome verifier from scratch (numpy/torch classification head); implement best-of-N pick-highest and top-k voting; contrast "random pick / majority vote / verifier pick"; reproduce the "candidates first rise then fall" curve.

### Paper 2: Let's Verify Step by Step (arXiv:2305.20050, lightman-verify-step-by-step.pdf)
- **Core idea**: systematically compare outcome supervision (ORM) and process supervision (PRM). ORM uses only final correctness as feedback; PRM gives feedback on **every step** of the reasoning chain. On the harder MATH dataset, process supervision substantially beats outcome supervision (PRM best-of-1860 reaches 78.2%). Releases PRM800K (800k step-level human labels). Compared with Uesato et al. 2022 (the two were comparable on grade-school math), this paper uses a stronger base, more human feedback, and a harder dataset.
- **Key formulas / algorithms**:
  - Generator: base GPT-4 → MathMix (light pretraining on 1.5B math-related tokens) → few-shot generate newline-separated step-by-step solutions, filter those with a correct final answer, then finetune 1 epoch only on "output format" (no new skills).
  - Data collection (PRM800K): humans label each step positive / negative / **neutral**; key design — **label only up to the first error step** (keeps outcome vs process supervision information comparable, and cuts labeling cost). Active learning: each round the current best PRM picks "high score but wrong answer" (convincing wrong-answer) solutions for annotators; iterate retraining the PRM; data efficiency about 2.6×.
  - ORM: same as Cobbe's token-level verifier, each token predicts whole-solution correctness probability, test uses the last-token score; training set is 100 uniform samples per problem (an order of magnitude larger than PRM800K).
  - PRM: at the last token of each step, predict the probability that the step is correct (a single special token, maximize log-likelihood), so it can use a standard LM pipeline; at test time **one forward pass** yields all step scores of the solution.
  - Solution-level reduction: most common is the product rule, $score(S)=\prod_i p(\text{step }i \text{ correct})$, i.e. probability that every step is correct; neutral treated as positive. (Table 4: product+neutral=positive 78.2% is best; min reduction / neutral=negative combinations 77.4–77.8%, small differences.)
  - Evaluation: best-of-N over uniform samples from the generator; test set 500 MATH problems (training expanded to 4.5K MATH test problems, leaving 500).
- **Key experimental results**:
  - best-of-1860: **PRM 78.2% vs ORM 72.4% vs majority voting 69.6%**, and the gap widens with N (PRM is better at searching among many candidates).
  - OOD generalization (latest AP Physics/Calculus/Chem + AMC10/12, 234 problems, best-of-100): PRM 72.9% vs ORM 63.8% vs majority 61.3%.
  - Small-model synthetic-supervision experiment (use PRMlarge as oracle to label a smaller model, controlled): process supervision beats both outcome-from-PRMlarge and outcome-from-final-answer at every data scale.
  - Credit-assignment explanation: ORM must infer "which step is wrong" itself, and on hard problems most generated solutions contain errors, so outcome negative labels carry little marginal information; PRM directly gives the location of the first wrong step.
  - Alignment: process supervision is a "negative alignment tax" — more interpretable, safer, and higher performing.
- **Relation to the lecture theme**: moves verification from "judge the outcome" to "judge the process", establishing the ORM vs PRM frame and PRM training/scoring details. PRM is the conceptual prototype of step-level reward in L6 RL, and the direct predecessor of Math-Shepherd (automatic labels) and GRPO (DAPO/DeepSeekMath use step rewards).
- **Demo-ready code points**: implement PRM step scoring and product/min reduction from scratch; implement best-of-N evaluation; simulate active-learning data picking of "label only to the first error"; heat-map each solution by step scores (which step pulls the score down).

### Paper 3: Math-Shepherd: Verify and Reinforce LLMs Step-by-step without Human Annotations (arXiv:2312.08935, math-shepherd.pdf)
- **Core idea**: PRM is strong but depends on expensive human step labels. This paper proposes **automatic process labeling**: inspired by MCTS, define "quality of a step" as "potential to reach the correct answer from this step". Given an intermediate step, a completer LLM continues N subsequent reasoning paths from that step, and the step is labeled by whether those paths' final answers equal the golden answer. The trained MATH-SHEPHERD can both rerank for best-of-N verification and do step-by-step PPO (a reward at the end of each step).
- **Key formulas / algorithms**:
  - Completer: from step $s_i$ continue $N$ paths $\{(s_{i+1,j},\dots,s_{K_j,j},a_j)\}_{j=1}^N$, $a_j$ the final answer of continuation $j$.
  - Hard Estimation (HE): $y^{HE}_{s_i} = \mathbb{1}[\exists j: a_j = a^*]$ (the step is good if any continuation reaches the correct answer).
  - Soft Estimation (SE): $y^{SE}_{s_i} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \mathbb{1}[a_j = a^*]$ (frequency of reaching the correct answer).
  - Training objective: PRM binary cross-entropy $L_{PRM}=\sum_i [y_{s_i}\log r_{s_i}+(1-y_{s_i})\log(1-r_{s_i})]$; by contrast ORM is a single whole-solution label $L_{ORM}=y_s\log r_s+(1-y_s)\log(1-r_s)$. The authors find binary vs Lightman's three-way classification differs little.
  - Verification ranking: solution score is the **min** of all step probabilities (following Lightman); combined with self-consistency: $a^*_{sc+rm}=\arg\max_a \sum_i \mathbb{1}[a_i=a]\cdot RM(p,S_i)$.
  - Step-by-step PPO: unlike traditional ORM-PPO which gives one reward at the end, each reasoning step gets a reward when it ends.
  - Data scale: generator and completer finetuned 3 epochs on MetaMATH; 7B/13B generators sample 15 solutions per problem after 1 epoch on GSM8K/MATH train, then dedup; completer is LLemma-7B, N=8 continuations. Yields ~170K GSM8K solutions, ~270K MATH solutions — about 4× PRM800K.
- **Key experimental results**:
  - Verification (best-of-256): DeepSeek-67B + SHEPHERD on GSM8K 93.3%, MATH 48.1%; LLaMA2-70B(MetaMATH): SC 88.0/39.4, ORM 91.8/40.4, SC+ORM 92.0/42.0, **SHEPHERD 93.2/44.5**, SC+SHEPHERD 92.4/45.2.
  - On open-source LLaMA models, **automatic labels beat human PRM800K on MATH** (main reason: PRM800K labels GPT-4 outputs, so there is a distribution gap with open-source solutions; automatic data is also 4× larger).
  - Label quality: 70B completer, N=4, HE agrees with human labels 86%; larger N introduces false positives; SE's distribution is closer to human but final verification performance is comparable to HE.
  - Vs NLI/rule automatic step labels (Li et al.): NLI(DeBERTa) 61.3, NLI(LLaMA2-13B) 75.6, Rule 75.0, **SHEPHERD(13B, N=4) 85.0**.
  - Step-by-step PPO (Mistral-7B): GSM8K 77.9→84.1, MATH 28.6→33.0; plus verification further to **89.1 / 43.5**. LLaMA2-7B: 66.6/19.2 →(+SHEPHERD-PPO) 73.2/21.6. Same-size comparison: +RFT 79.0/29.9, +ORM-PPO 81.8/31.3 (step supervision > outcome supervision).
  - Data efficiency: at 10k training instances PRM is already ~4pt above ORM, and PRM's ceiling is higher.
  - OOD (Hungarian national exam, 33 problems, 100 points max): greedy 46.0, ORM 54.0, **SHEPHERD 63.0**.
  - Scaling: a large verifier checking a small generator lifts substantially; a small verifier checking a large generator falls below self-consistency — the verifier should be stronger than the generator. The completer needs to have "seen" the problem distribution (a Weak train set that drops related problems performs poorly).
- **Relation to the lecture theme**: turns PRM from "human-label monopoly" into "automatically scalable", closing the "verification → RL" loop; the most direct predecessor of L6 DeepSeekMath/GRPO (step-level reward training). The quality–cost tradeoff of automatic labels (N, HE vs SE, completer strength) is an experiment the notebook can run.
- **Demo-ready code points**: implement HE/SE automatic labeling from scratch (scripted completer); implement min reduction + self-consistency combined selection; hand-calculate reward generation for step-by-step PPO; plot "verifier-selected answer vs majority vote" vs N.

### Paper 4: Shrinking the Generation-Verification Gap with Weak Verifiers (Weaver, arXiv:2506.18203, shrinking-gen-verif-gap.pdf)
- **⚠️ File issue**: the actual content of `papers/lecture-03/shrinking-gen-verif-gap.pdf` is not this paper, but arXiv:2504.18514, the cosmology paper "Inflationary background renormalization" (Kristiano & Yokoyama, unrelated to verification). The real paper's arXiv number is **2506.18203** (NeurIPS 2025, Stanford + UW-Madison + Together AI, Jon Saad-Falcon et al.). The notes below are based on the real 2506.18203. Re-download the correct PDF when writing the notebook.
- **Core idea**: take the previous papers' idea to the end — **formalize the generation–verification gap** (gap = Pass@K − Success Rate), and note that "a single weak verifier is unreliable" is not the end: combine many weak verifiers (reward models + LM judges, 7B–72B) with **weak supervision** (no human labels) as a weighted ensemble, approaching oracle, even letting a cheap non-reasoning model (Llama3.3-70B Instruct + 33 verifiers ≤72B) reach o3-mini level on math/science (87.7% vs 86.7%), realizing the GPT-4o → o3-mini jump "by test-time sampling rather than finetuning".
- **Key formulas / algorithms**:
  - Gap definition: Gap = Pass@K − Success Rate. Example: Llama3.3-70B on GPQA Diamond, Pass@100 (oracle) = 82.8%, but majority vote only 45.5% (37.3pt gap); a single verifier's correctness rate is only 43–62%.
  - Normalize + binarize: verifier outputs differ (logits / binary / Likert); first normalize, then binarize into votes $\bar{s}_{ijk}\in\{0,1\}$, and filter low-quality / uninformative verifiers.
  - Latent-variable model (naive Bayes): whether a solution is correct $Y\in\{0,1\}$ is latent; verifier outputs are conditionally independent given $Y$; each verifier has TPR $w_{k,1}=P(S_k{=}1|Y{=}1)$ and TNR $w_{k,0}=P(S_k{=}0|Y{=}0)$.
  - Method of moments (no labels): match observable moments to model moments — for (i,j): $P(S_i,S_j)=\sum_Y P(S_i|Y)P(S_j|Y)P(Y)$, univariate: $P(S_i{=}1)=P(S_i{=}1|Y{=}1)P(Y{=}1)+P(S_i{=}1|Y{=}0)P(Y{=}0)$. Objective $\min_\mu \|O_{off\text{-}diag}-(\mu P\mu^\top)_{off\text{-}diag}\|^2+\|\mathrm{diag}(O)-\mu P\mathbb{1}^\top\|^2$, solved by gradient descent.
  - Inference: $P(Y{=}1|\bar{s})\propto P(Y{=}1)\prod_k P(S_k{=}\bar{s}_k|Y{=}1)$, pick the solution with max posterior per problem. Needs only a tiny dev set (about 1% of the test set, 5–10 Q&A) to estimate task difficulty $P(Y{=}1)$.
  - Distillation: use Weaver's posterior scores as pseudo-labels, distill into a 400M ModernBERT-Large cross-encoder.
- **Key experimental results**:
  - Main result (Llama3.3-70B Instruct generation, 33 verifiers, K=100): **Weaver 87.7%** vs majority vote 72.2, strongest single RM 72.7, naive (equal-weight) ensemble 69.1, self-verification 66.9, multi-agent verification 71.6; controls o3-mini 86.7, GPT-4o 69.0, oracle Pass@100 91.9. By dataset: MATH500 93.4, GPQA Diamond 72.1, MMLU-College 94.9, MMLU-Pro 90.2.
  - Relative lift: +17.1% vs equal-weight average, +13.5% vs majority vote; Pass@1 lift +17.9 for 8B models, +14.5 for 70B.
  - Weighted ensemble (oracle) is up to 11.2pt above equal-weight average; with only 1% labels, supervised-learning weights drop 20.1pt on average → that is why weak supervision is required.
  - Distilled 400M: keeps 98.7% accuracy, verification compute down 99.97% (35.35→1.01 exaFLOPs/query); 23.2pt above majority vote, adding only about 0.57% inference cost, runnable on one GPU.
  - Scaling: majority vote plateaus around 2^2–2^3 exaFLOPs/query; Weaver can keep pushing to 512 exaFLOPs.
  - Weak-to-strong: 8B generator + 8B verifiers with Weaver reach 70.0%, near the 70B majority-vote baseline (71.6%).
  - Failure mode: on MiniF2F, AIMO, and other tasks where correct answers are rare, Weaver's advantage is limited (almost no correct solutions are sampled, so verifiers have nothing to work with).
- **Relation to the lecture theme**: the lecture's closing "combine verifiers with sampling". Answers the final question "a single verifier is not strong enough, human labels are too expensive": weak supervision + ensemble + distillation is the engineering path to robust verification; also turns "generation is easy, verification is hard" from intuition into a measurable, optimizable gap. Previews L6: reward-signal quality sets the RL ceiling (reward hacking / over-optimization are themes of L9 and alignment).
- **Demo-ready code points**: implement method-of-moments estimation of TPR/TNR for several simulated weak verifiers from scratch; implement naive-Bayes posterior aggregation to pick a solution; contrast unweighted / weighted / majority aggregation as candidate count K grows; plot the Pass@K vs Success Rate gap curve.

## Teaching thread (how a Stanford instructor might teach this)

Organized as intuition → hand calculation → code → experiment; the four papers are four advances of the same story:

1. **A failure case sets motivation (generation is easy, verification is hard)**. First recap L2 self-consistency and best-of-N, then a striking fact: the correct answer is already in the candidate pool (Pass@100 is high), but majority vote cannot pick it (GPQA 82.8% vs 45.5%). Analogy: the student can write the correct answer, but the grader (verifier) is too weak and scores every correct one low. Autoregressive generation cannot look back; one wrong step loses the whole solution. Here we give the lecture's core definition: generation–verification gap = Pass@K − Success Rate.
2. **Cobbe: the most basic verifier**. Since answers can be auto-graded (GSM8K has a unique answer), label generated solutions by final answer, train an "outcome verifier", and at test time best-of-N pick the highest score. Hand-calculate the training pipeline once (generator 2 epochs → sample 100 → verifier 1 epoch). Two conceptual holes: ① solution-level vs token-level (token-level = a value function per token, introducing "scores can be viewed by position"); ② outcome supervision can be fooled by lucky correct answers (wrong reasoning, right answer = false positive). Experimental shock: 6B verification ≈ 175B finetune (30×).
3. **Lightman: looking only at the outcome is not enough; look at the process**. Credit assignment: ORM must guess which step is wrong, and generated solutions on hard problems mostly contain errors, so outcome negative labels carry little information. PRM has humans label each step (positive/negative/neutral), only up to the first error. Hand-calculate PRM scoring: product rule (probability every step is correct), neutral as positive. Number contrast (78.2 vs 72.4 vs 69.6) and stress that "the gap widens with N". **Where readers stall**: confusing Cobbe's token-level with PRM — the former predicts *whole-solution* correctness at every token (the same label painted over the whole solution); the latter has a *separate* label per step.
4. **Math-Shepherd: automate the human labels**. Human step labels are unrealistically expensive (PRM800K is 800k human labels). Change the key intuition: do not ask "is this step itself correct", ask "if we continue from this step, can we still reach the correct answer" — MCTS-style rollout. Hand-calculate HE and SE: any continuation hitting is HE=1; SE is the hit rate. Make three knobs clear: completer strength, continuation count N, HE vs SE. Numbers: 86% agreement with humans, automatic data beats PRM800K, step-by-step PPO takes Mistral-7B to 84.1/33.0.
5. **Weaver: a single verifier is weak, so twist many weak verifiers into a strong one**. Close by returning to the gap definition in step 1: verifier correctness is only 43–62%, but 33 weak verifiers with weak-supervision weighted ensemble approach oracle (87.7% ≈ o3-mini). Hand-calculate the method-of-moments intuition: how much two verifiers agree on the same solution hides their respective accuracies (TPR/TNR estimable without labels), then naive-Bayes aggregation. Explain why "equal-weight average" is weak — verifier quality varies a lot.
6. **Synthesis and preview**: one axis of verification-signal sources — ground-truth checks (executor / final answer) → outcome supervision → process supervision → weak-verifier ensemble, cost rising and robustness rising. Preview L4's "code-execution feedback" as another harder verification path; L6 GRPO/PPO will use this lecture's reward model as a training signal; reward quality decides RL success.

Key comparison table (usable as a notebook summary cell):
| Paper | Supervision signal | Label source | Solution score | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cobbe | Outcome (whole solution) | Automatic (final answer) | Last-token score | best-of-N verification |
| Lightman | Process (each step) | Human (PRM800K) | Product rule | best-of-N, active learning |
| Math-Shepherd | Process (each step) | Automatic (completer+HE/SE) | Min rule | verification + step-by-step PPO |
| Weaver | Multi-verifier ensemble | Unlabeled (method of moments) | Naive-Bayes posterior | weak-verifier aggregation + distillation |

## Code demo ideas (3–6)

1. **Mini math dataset + outcome verifier (ORM)**: with numpy/torch, make a controllable "answerer" (e.g. rule-generated arithmetic problems + solution strings with random error steps), train a small verifier (MLP or linear classification head, input = solution-string features, label = whether the final answer is correct). Then implement best-of-N: N candidates per problem, compare three selectors "random / majority vote (self-consistency) / verifier pick highest". Expected output: verifier selection rises stably with N; majority plateaus after some N; intuition of Cobbe figure 7.
2. **Hand-calculate PRM step reward**: given a two/three-step solution (hand-constructed, with one intermediate error), define per-step correctness probabilities by rule, implement product and min reductions, and demonstrate neutral→positive mapping. Hand-calculate and visualize a heat map of each step's score (which step pulls the whole-solution score down). Expected output: the error step's score is clearly low; product score is clearly below a correct solution; contrast ranking of Lightman Table 4's four scoring strategies.
3. **Simulate Math-Shepherd automatic labels (HE vs SE)**: a scripted/rule "completer" continues N paths from an intermediate step (pseudo-random + known golden answer for controllable results), implement HE ($\exists$ hit) and SE (hit rate), contrast ranking of verifiers trained on the two labels; vary N=1,4,16 and watch label quality. Expected output: HE at small N reproduces the 86% agreement-with-human intuition; at large N, SE is closer to the true distribution (paper Figure 4).
4. **Verifier-selected answer vs majority-vote curves**: fix a generator (scripted), on a GSM8K-style small sample plot "selection accuracy vs candidate count N": three lines self-consistency, ORM (outcome verifier), PRM (step-level, min reduction). Expected output: the PRM line has a steeper slope as N grows, and the gap vs majority vote widens — reproduce Lightman Figure 3 and Math-Shepherd Figure 3.
5. **Generation–verification gap visualization**: define and compute Pass@K (fraction of problems whose candidate pool contains a correct solution, oracle metric) and Success Rate (probability the selector actually picks correctly); for several selectors plot gap = Pass@K − SuccessRate vs K. Expected output: majority vote's gap first falls then rises with K (plateau), showing "the correct solution was generated but not selected" as the lecture's root.
6. **Mini Weaver: unlabeled method of moments + weak-verifier ensemble**: construct 3–5 "simulated weak verifiers" with different TPR/TNR (0/1 votes on a batch of solutions, with noise), provide no labels, estimate each verifier's accuracy from pairwise agreement by method of moments, then implement naive-Bayes posterior selection; contrast majority, equal-weight average, and estimated weighted aggregation as K grows. Expected output: estimated weights substantially beat equal-weight average and majority, and approach oracle accuracy without labels (Weaver Figure 2/3 and the 87.7% story).

## Exercise ideas (3)

1. **PRM solution-score reduction**: given a step-level probability array (e.g. `[0.9, 0.8, 0.2, 0.9]`, step 3 is wrong), fill in `score_product` and `score_min` (and explain treating neutral as positive). `assert score_min(probs) == 0.2` and `assert abs(score_product(probs) - 0.9*0.8*0.2*0.9) < 1e-9`. Hint: product penalizes long solutions; min watches only the worst step; when both problems are correct, which reduction is more stable?
2. **Math-Shepherd HE vs SE**: given whether 8 continuation paths of one step end correctly (e.g. `answers = [True, True, False, False, True, False, False, False]`), fill in HE (1 if any True) and SE (True fraction). `assert hard_estimation(answers) == 1`, `assert abs(soft_estimation(answers) - 3/8) < 1e-9`. Hint: SE is frequency, HE is existence; smaller N makes SE noisier.
3. **Verifier vs majority vote in best-of-N**: given a constructed candidate list (each item `(verifier_score, final_answer, is_correct)`, answers take only two values A/B), fill in two selectors "verifier picks highest score" and "majority vote picks the majority answer", and count who is more accurate on the given list. `assert choose_by_verifier(candidates) == "A"`, `assert choose_by_majority(candidates) == "B"`. Hint: majority vote only counts answer occurrences and ignores each solution's verification score; when "the wrong answer is the majority but verification scores are low", the verifier wins.
