## Lecture theme

This lecture answers a core question: **why mathematics is the touchstone for "AI reasoning", and which combination of techniques lets AI win medals at the IMO.** Three properties of contest math make it an ideal touchstone for agent reasoning: (1) **verifiability** — whether a proof is correct has an objective criterion; one cannot bluff with "it looks reasonable", in sharp contrast to the fuzzy evaluation of open-domain dialogue; (2) **sparse reward** — either the proof is found (full 7 points) or it is not (0 points), with no graded intermediate reward, so training signal is extremely scarce; (3) **search and tools are required** — a single generation almost surely fails; one must search a huge proof space, propose new auxiliary constructions, and have a formal verifier keep watch.

This lecture sits in the middle of Part 4 Frontiers, right after L12 (LLM reasoning: CoT, self-consistency, reasoning models). It **assembles every earlier building block into a complete system that pursues "superhuman reasoning"**: L02's test-time compute scaling (spend more compute at inference for a better result), L03's verifier (here upgraded to a **formal, reliable Lean verifier**, not LLM-as-judge), L08's search (AlphaCode's three-piece kit replayed in proof space), L07's open-ended evolution / RL self-improvement (the AlphaZero self-play paradigm). The whole lecture is threaded by the IMO line: AlphaGeometry (geometry, neuro-symbolic) → AlphaProof (formal proof + RL) → Gemini Deep Think taking IMO 2025 gold (a pure natural-language route).

## Close reading

### Paper 1: AlphaGeometry: An Olympiad-level AI system for geometry (Nature, 2024, DOI:10.1038/s41586-023-06747-5, no arxiv; materials in alphageometry-readme.md)

- **Core idea**: split geometric proof into a **neuro-symbolic two-stage** process. The symbolic engine DDAR does deterministic reasoning — combining Deductive Database (DD, Horn-clause-style deduction rules, e.g. "the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal") with Algebraic Reasoning (AR, solving angle / ratio / distance equations). But DDAR has a fatal shortfall: on problems that need **auxiliary constructions** it stalls completely. AlphaGeometry uses a 150M-parameter decoder-only transformer (JAX + beam search) that, when DDAR is stuck, **proposes what auxiliary point / line to add next**, then hands control back to DDAR. Auxiliary construction is equivalent to "generating an external term", the hardest cognitive move in contest geometry, and the key of the whole system: "use learning to patch what the symbolic engine lacks".

- **Key formulas / algorithms**:
  - **Data synthesis**: sample from about 1 billion random theorem premises, use the symbolic engine to generate about 100 million synthetic theorems and proofs (many proofs >200 steps, 4× the length of an average contest proof). The LM is first pretrained on the synthetic data, then fine-tuned on about 9 million proofs that need auxiliary constructions (about 9%) — so the model learns "in what situation to add what construction".
  - **Inference**: the trained LM supplies auxiliary-construction candidates, DDAR verifies and advances; iterate until a proof is found. Public reproduction parameters: `BATCH_SIZE=32, BEAM_SIZE=512, DEPTH=16`.
  - **Hardware**: 4 V100s + 250 CPU workers (to meet IMO time limits).

- **Key experimental results**:
  - IMO-AG-30 (30 historical IMO geometry problems) solves **25/30**; DDAR alone only 14; the previous best Wu method only 10; near average gold-medal level.
  - On jgex_ag_231, from 198 to 228.
  - Outputs **human-readable proofs**, solves all geometry problems from IMO 2000 and 2015; also finds a generalization of an IMO 2004 theorem (identifies an unused premise in the proof).

- **Relation to the lecture theme**: this is the first foundation stone of this lecture's "neuro-symbolic combination" — **the neural net is responsible for "proposing" (producing candidate steps), the symbolic engine for "verifying" (deterministic reasoning and search)**. It directly echoes L03's answer verification: here the verifier is formal and reliable, and the model can never "bluff" it. It is also the structural predecessor of AlphaProof: the same "model proposes candidates + a reliable verifier keeps watch", only the symbolic engine is replaced by a formal proof system.

- **Demo-ready code points**:
  - Hand-write in Python a mini symbolic engine of **single-step deduction rules** (e.g. equal angles, triangle-congruence criteria), deriving new facts step by step from known conditions.
  - Demo "auxiliary construction": the same geometric configuration, without an auxiliary point vs with one; the difference in how many conclusions can be derived — a small enumeration that lets us see "stuck" vs "unlocked" intuitively.

### Paper 2: AlphaProof / AlphaGeometry 2 — DeepMind blog ("AI achieves silver-medal standard solving International Mathematical Olympiad problems", 2024-07-25; materials in alphaproof-blog.md; technical details later published in Nature, 2025, DOI:10.1038/s41586-025-09833-y)

- **Core idea**: AlphaProof treats **mathematical proof as a game**, using AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning to search for proofs inside the Lean formal proof assistant. Lean's benefit is that **the reasoning process can be machine-verified line by line** — the verifier is absolutely reliable, solving two problems at once: verifiability (no hallucination) and training signal (every verified proof is usable supervision / reward). The bottleneck is that human-written formal data is extremely scarce; the solution is to fine-tune Gemini into an **auto-formalizer** that automatically translates natural-language problems into formal statements, building a large problem bank of about 1 million problems of varying difficulty, so the system "self-plays" to strengthen itself on hard problems.

- **Key formulas / algorithms** (combined with the Nature 2025 full-paper details):
  - **Training pipeline**: about 100 million informal problems → Gemini formalizer translates into a formal language (about 1 million source problems expand into about 80 million formal propositions) → a solver network searches for proofs or counterexamples in Lean → AlphaZero gradually self-strengthens, problem difficulty increasing. Pretrain on about 300B tokens of math and code text, then supervised fine-tune on about 300k human proofs in Mathlib.
  - **Proof network**: an about 3B-parameter encoder-decoder transformer that reads the current proof state and outputs two things: which tactic to try next, and an estimate of "how many steps remain" (used to allocate search compute intelligently). Search is AlphaZero-inspired tree search, including an **AND-OR tree** (decompose mutually independent subgoals) and **progressive sampling** (broader tactic exploration on the critical path).
  - **Training cost**: the main RL stage about 80k TPU-days, formalization itself about 100k TPU-days (this is the "do not expect to reproduce without large-scale training" part).
  - **TTRL (test-time reinforcement learning)** — the key innovation for super-hard problems: for the target problem, use Gemini (few-shot + up to 15 rounds of evolution) to **self-generate about 400k variants** (simplify, generalize, analogize, programmatically modify hypotheses / goals), then run a dedicated AlphaZero-style RL loop on that variant set, training an "expert" model that accumulates the insights needed to solve. A single TTRL run takes 2–3 days of compute, far beyond the human 4.5-hour limit, but it is the key to cracking IMO 2024 problem 6 (only 5 contestants solved it).
  - **AlphaGeometry 2**: a large upgrade of the previous generation — language model trained from scratch, an order of magnitude more synthetic data, two orders of magnitude faster symbolic engine, plus a **knowledge-sharing** mechanism (combine different search trees to solve harder problems), able to handle object motion, angle / ratio / distance equations, and other hard problems. Before the contest it could solve 83% of IMO historical geometry problems from the past 25 years (previous generation 53%).

- **Key experimental results**:
  - IMO 2024: 4 of 6 problems solved (AlphaProof 2 algebra + 1 number theory; AlphaGeometry 2 1 geometry), total **28/42 = silver** (gold line 29 points, 58 of 609 contestants reached it). The hardest problem was solved by only 5 human contestants; AlphaProof solved it too. Scores were judged under IMO rules by Fields medalist Timothy Gowers and others.
  - Solve time from a few minutes to at most three days (humans have two 4.5-hour sittings).
  - Formal benchmarks: on the corrected miniF2F test set **96.3%** (minimal search) / **99.6%** (TTRL); new benchmark formal-imo (258 historical non-geometry IMO problems) 58.3%; PutnamBench-test 56.1%.
  - Known limits: depends on the evolving Lean ecosystem (Mathlib); hard to handle problems that "define a new concept"; combinatorics problems are largely unsolved; some contest problems still need human translation into Lean; huge compute (core team about 10 people; the variant-generation method was designed by IMO gold medalist Miklós Horváth).

- **Relation to the lecture theme**: this paper is the hub of the whole lecture, twisting "verifier + search + reinforcement learning" into one strand: the verifier here is a **formal, reliable Lean checker** (again distinct from LLM-as-judge); search is AlphaZero-style proof-tree search (isomorphic to L08's AlphaCode generate-filter-cluster: a mass of candidates + reliable screening); RL is self-play iterative improvement (in a line with L07's open-ended evolution). TTRL then pushes "spend more compute at inference" (L02) to an extreme — even **training** is moved to test time for a single problem.

- **Demo-ready code points**:
  - Write in Python a **minimal natural-deduction verifier** (a few inference rules of propositional logic), showing that "every step of a formal proof can be machine-checked".
  - Implement an abstract **search + verifier** loop: generate candidate proof steps → verifier checks → if they pass, advance / if they fail, backtrack, reproducing AlphaProof's search skeleton (small search space, no real Lean).

### Paper 3: Gemini Deep Think takes IMO 2025 gold (DeepMind blog, 2025-07; "Advanced version of Gemini with Deep Think officially achieves gold-medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad")

- **Core idea**: **drop formalization entirely, take a pure natural-language end-to-end route.** Gemini Deep Think reads the official problem statements directly, writes rigorous proofs in natural language, **finishes within the 4.5-hour contest time limit**, and scores **35/42 (5 of 6 problems correct)**, reaching the gold line. Scores were **officially certified** by IMO coordinators under the same standard as human contestants (DeepMind was among the first AI cohorts to receive official certification). IMO president Gregor Dolinar confirmed: "35 points — a gold-medal score, solutions clear and precise, mostly easy to follow."

- **Key algorithms / practices**:
  - **Training**: new reinforcement-learning techniques, using multi-step reasoning, problem-solving, and theorem-proving data, plus a curated high-quality math solution set and IMO-specific prompts (hints).
  - **Parallel thinking**: explore and combine several possible solution paths at once, rather than walking a single linear chain of thought to the end — equivalent to internalizing "search / branching" into the decode process.
  - **Contrast with 2024**: IMO 2024's 28/42 (silver) needed formal translation + 2–3 days of compute; 2025's 35/42 (gold) was finished in natural language within the time limit. This is the confluence of the same guest's two technical routes.

- **Key experimental results and anecdotes**:
  - The only unsolved problem, number 6, was exactly the hardest of the contest (only 5 human contestants solved it); the model started from a wrong assumption.
  - On a number-theory problem, the model gave an elegant elementary-number-theory proof, while many human contestants used graduate-level Dirichlet's theorem.
  - Later progress (reported 2026-02): this direction has advanced to **research-level mathematics** — a research agent named Aletheia has automatically solved open problems, scored about 90% on IMO-ProofBench Advanced, and contributed several papers.
  - An institutional detail: contemporaneous OpenAI claims of "gold-medal level" were scored by former IMO medalists it hired itself, without IMO official certification; Thang Luong remarked that "a result that did not go through that process might be one point away from silver".

- **Relation to the lecture theme**: this paper is the **present peak** of the "superhuman reasoning" narrative, answering whether there is a simpler path besides neuro-symbolic: when LLM + RL is strong enough, perhaps one does not need the insurance of formalization to produce trustworthy, gradeable mathematical proofs. It forms a methodological contrast with the first two papers — **the formal-verification route (reliable but expensive and slow) vs the pure natural-language RL route (fast and general, but verification remains an open problem)**. For the Agent course: mathematics is still the touchstone, but the agent-core ability "reliable verification" takes completely different forms on the two routes.

## Teaching thread (how a Stanford instructor might teach this)

1. **Why mathematics is the touchstone of reasoning.** First give a failure case: let an ordinary LLM attempt an IMO problem, outputting a proof that "looks quite reasonable" but hides a flaw — because natural-language mathematics **has no machine-checkable verifier**, errors are hard to find. From this define two things: mathematics' **verifiability** (right/wrong is objectively decidable) and **sparse reward** (full marks or zero, no graded intermediate signal). Raise the lecture's dilemma: how to find a correct path in an environment of "huge proof space, extremely sparse signal". The instructor's analogy: a math contest is an exam scored by an "absolutely fair and never-erring" referee, while ordinary dialogue tasks have no such referee.

2. **AlphaGeometry: the first success of neuro-symbolic combination.** First say what the symbolic engine DDAR can do (deterministic deduction: DD rules + AR algebra), then show where it stalls — **auxiliary constructions**. Hand-calculate / visualize an example: a configuration without an auxiliary point cannot be pushed; after adding "the segment joining two points", congruent triangles follow at once. This introduces the lecture's first reusable pattern: **the neural net proposes candidates (auxiliary constructions / next step), the symbolic engine deterministically verifies and advances**. In data synthesis, make clear "why 100 million synthetic theorems" — human proofs are too few, so the symbolic engine must make its own training data. Stall warning: readers easily confuse "the LM outputs a stretch of reasoning" with "the LM proposes a construction, the engine derives" — the former has no verifier, the latter is guaranteed by the engine at every step.

3. **AlphaProof: turn proof into a game.** Starting from AlphaGeometry's limits (geometry only, the symbolic engine's domain is hard-coded), introduce the formal language Lean: it turns "an arbitrary mathematical proposition + proof" into an object a machine can check line by line. Then mathematical proof = AlphaZero's board game: the current proof state is the position, a tactic is a move, the Lean checker is the rules. Make the **formalizer bottleneck and solution** clear: human formal data is too scarce → Gemini automatically translates natural-language problems into formal propositions → a problem bank of tens of millions → the system self-plays on hard problems. Then TTRL: facing a super-hard problem, even training is moved to test time — self-generate 400k variants for a single problem and train an "expert"; that is the solution of IMO 2024's hardest problem. Stall warning: readers often mistake AlphaProof for "a trained model that directly outputs a proof"; it is actually "model + large-scale search + test-time RL, three pieces". They also easily mix "natural-language mathematics" with "formal mathematics" — the former is verified by humans, the latter by machines.

4. **IMO 2025 gold: Gemini's route.** Pull the camera back to the present: no formalization needed, pure natural language + reinforcement learning + parallel thinking, an officially certified gold medal within 4.5 hours. Contrast with 2024 (28 points, 2–3 days, needed translation into Lean), making each route's trade-off clear — **the formal-verification route pursues "absolute reliability", the natural-language RL route pursues "fast and general"**. Close by stringing the three papers into one evolution line of "toward superhuman reasoning": symbolic engine → neuro-symbolic → formalization + RL → pure natural language, and point out mathematics' meaning as an agent touchstone: any agent that wants to get stronger at reasoning can use "verifiable mathematical tasks" as the range for training and evaluation (echoing L12's reasoning models, and planting a "reliability" seed for L15's autonomous agents).

## Code demo ideas (4–6)

1. **Single-step reasoning of a mini geometry symbolic engine**: hand-write in Python 3–5 geometric deduction rules (e.g. "base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal", "triangle angle sum 180°", "corresponding sides of congruent triangles are equal"), input a set of known conditions (known angle / segment relations), loop applying rules until no new facts can be derived. Expected output: print step by step "from X derived Y (by rule Z)", and derivation depth. Use `torch.manual_seed(42)` for reproducibility.

2. **Auxiliary construction unlocking derivation**: the same geometric configuration, two versions of constructing the external term "auxiliary point / line" (without vs adding a point and declaring an edge relation), contrast the size of the derivable-conclusion sets. Intuitively see "stuck vs unlocked", and note that auxiliary construction ≈ generating an external term in search. Expected output: two conclusion sets + a count contrast; after adding the auxiliary point, 1–2 extra key facts are derived.

3. **A minimal natural-deduction verifier (the smallest example of a formal proof)**: implement in Python a few zeroth-order inference rules of propositional logic (conjunction introduction / elimination, implication elimination modus ponens, proof by contradiction), write a `verify(proof)` that checks line by line whether each step follows from earlier lines by a legal rule. Expected output: a legal proof passes; after a human inserts an illegal step, it errors and reports the line number. This demo emphasizes "formalization = every step can be machine-checked", contrasting LLM natural-language argument.

4. **An abstract search + verifier framework**: implement a general search-and-verify loop — `Generator` (from the current state generate candidate next steps, by rule enumeration or `llm_client`) + `Verifier` (check whether a candidate is legal / advances) + simple DFS/BFS or beam. Use it to solve a small goal (e.g. "from a set of axioms derive a target proposition"), reproducing AlphaProof's search skeleton (candidate → verify → advance → backtrack). Expected output: search-tree path, nodes visited, steps used to find a legal proof.

5. **Use llm_client to let an LLM take one proof step + manual verification**: with `get_llm(force_scripted=True)` (scripted mode outputs a scripted placeholder) let the LLM generate proof steps for a small problem, then feed each step to demo 3's verifier, showing the neuro-symbolic division of labor "**LLM proposes candidates, a reliable verifier keeps watch**". Scripted output must be parseable: have the scripted output carry `STEP: ... RULE: ...` tags so the verifier can digest line by line. Expected output: a pass/fail report of line-by-line verification, noting that scripted-mode output is a placeholder.

6. **A small contrast of formal vs natural language**: the same problem, place the LLM's natural-language answer beside a "stepwise formalization + machine verification" version, count examples of "natural language looks right but formal verification fails" (scripted results in the scripted track). Expected output: a contrast table + the conclusion "verifiability is the means of keeping vague argument out".

## Exercise ideas (3)

1. **Implement verification of one deduction rule**: examines "verifier" logic. Given a simple proof (several lines: premise / conclusion + rule name) and a rule library, write `check_line(proof, i)` that judges whether line i is legally derived from earlier lines by a rule; after filling in, assert: a legal proof all passes, and after a human corrupts a line's premises that line is judged illegal. Hint: first parse line i's premise line numbers, then match against the rule library.

2. **Implement a search-verify loop that finds a proof**: examines the "search + verifier" framework. Given a set of axioms, a goal proposition, and a candidate-generation function (returns all legally applicable rule instances), write `find_proof(axioms, goal, max_depth)` that uses DFS to find a proof path; assert: every step of the found proof verifies as legal, and the conclusion equals the goal. Hint: maintain a "currently known fact set", each step pick an applicable rule from it.

3. **LLM proof collaborating with a verifier**: examines "neuro-symbolic division of labor". Given a stretch of LLM output tagged `STEP:.../RULE:...` (a scripted trajectory), write `parse_steps(text)` that parses steps, then check each with the verifier; assert: the parsed step count matches the tag-pair count, and the verification report marks failing step numbers. Hint: use `re.findall` to split by tags, feed line by line to exercise 1's verifier.
